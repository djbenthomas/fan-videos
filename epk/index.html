Why a Nashville band is a different animal

Nashville's first-call players have been called the "A-Team" since the late 1950s — a small circle of musicians who worked several sessions a day, for different artists and labels, and shaped the sound of the records everybody knows. The bar that built that reputation is still the bar in this town: players who "read an arrangement quickly, adapt on the fly, and deliver a usable take in only a few tries."

— The Little Nashville Opry, on Nashville's session players

Lower Broadway is where that standard gets tested in public — four-hour sets, five nights a week, no rehearsal, a crowd two feet from the stage that has heard every one of these songs a hundred times. A band that survives that room can walk into any room.

  • Three hours of live music — two sets with a break, or continuous if the room is moving
  • Set list built around the membership — Merle and Hank to current country, plus the classic rock everybody actually dances to
  • Dressed in our best country wear — boots, hats, the works, and still club-appropriate
  • Sound check in the afternoon, off the dance floor before guests arrive
  • Requests welcome and usually played
Nashville isn't where you go to be a big fish. It's where the pond is full of sharks — and the bands that work here every night learn to swim.
